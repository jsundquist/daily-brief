# Newsletter ingestion without Gmail

Ticket: [04-newsletter-ingestion](../issues/04-newsletter-ingestion.md). Researched 2026-11-07.

## Bottom line

Use a three-tier ingestion order, no Gmail anywhere:

1. **Publisher RSS/Atom feed** (primary). It is the platform's own offered, public interface, so it carries no scraping or ToS risk. It works well for free posts on Substack, Ghost and Buttondown; beehiiv needs a per-publication feed URL.
2. **Dedicated inbox via a catch-all address** (for paid, private or feed-less newsletters). Subscribe a project-owned address, receive mail through an inbound-email service, and parse the MIME. This is the only route that reliably gets the full text of paid issues the user pays for.
3. **Kill-the-Newsletter** (convenience fallback). It turns an email address into an Atom feed. Use the hosted instance for low-stakes sources only, or self-host (MIT).

**Do not scrape HTML archives.** Substack's and beehiiv's Terms prohibit it outright, and it is brittle. Feeds and a dedicated inbox cover everything scraping would.

Findings and caveats:

- **Substack paid posts.** Only third-party sources say paid posts appear as a preview in the feed. No primary source confirmed it. Treat paid Substack content as unavailable via RSS until tested.
- **Substack feed length.** The roughly-20-item figure comes from a single low-quality source. Verify it before relying on it. Polling daily makes length a minor risk either way.
- **beehiiv feeds are opt-in.** The author must enable the feed and its URL is not guessable from the publication slug. Some publications will have no feed. beehiiv's docs do not say whether the feed has full text or excerpts. The display options in that article apply to RSS ingestion (incoming feeds), not the outgoing feed.
- **Buttondown feeds depend on the author.** The feed exists only if the author enabled web archives.
- **Ghost Content API.** Needs a key the site owner creates, so it is usable only for sites you control. The public `/rss/` feed is the right interface for third-party newsletters. Ghost says the API is cacheable and can be fetched "as often as you like," but gives no numeric rate limit.
- **Feed discovery.** Buttondown archive pages advertise the feed via discovery tags (per search results on its changelog; not verified on the page itself). A one-time `<link rel="alternate">` lookup per source is enough; no discovery is needed at run time.

## 2. Scraping constraints

- **robots.txt is advisory, not authorization.** [RFC 9309](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9309) states "These rules are not a form of access authorization." Crawlers should not use a cached robots.txt for more than 24 hours. A 4xx on robots.txt means the crawler may access anything; a 5xx means it must assume complete disallow. An unattended job should implement both rules.
- **Substack robots.txt** ([substack.com/robots.txt](https://substack.com/robots.txt)) disallows only private feeds (`/feed/private`, `/feed/podcast/*/private/*.rss`), `/action/` and comment pages (`/p/*/comment/*`), plus a full block for BLEXBot. Public `/feed`, `/archive` and posts are not disallowed. There are no AI-crawler-specific rules.
- **Substack ToS** ([substack.com/tos](https://substack.com/tos)) says users may not "crawl," "scrape," or "spider" any page, data or portion of Substack, manually or automatically. It also bars copying or storing a significant portion of content and putting unreasonable load on infrastructure. It sets no numeric rate limit. So robots.txt permits `/archive` while the ToS prohibits scraping it; the ToS is the stricter constraint.
- **beehiiv ToS** ([beehiiv.com/tou](https://www.beehiiv.com/tou)) prohibits "data mining, robots, crawling, scraping or data gathering or extraction methods" and forbids circumventing blocks (IP masking, proxies). I did not fetch beehiiv's robots.txt.
- **Not checked:** the Ghost, Buttondown and Kill-the-Newsletter terms of service, and the robots.txt files for Ghost, Buttondown and beehiiv. Self-hosted Ghost sites set their own policy.
- **Paywalls.** A scraper without a logged-in session gets previews only, and logging in programmatically to read paid content is the circumvention that terms like beehiiv's target. Treat paywalled full text as reachable only via the user's own subscription delivered by email (tier 2).
- **Conclusion.** Reading a feed the publisher offers is not scraping. Fetching HTML archive pages is, and the platform terms above forbid it for Substack and beehiiv. Do not build scraping into v1.

## 3. Dedicated inbox / forwarding address

This is the route for paid newsletters, platforms with no feed, and anything the author did not expose.

- Create a project-owned address (a domain the user controls), subscribe it to the newsletters, and have an inbound-email service deliver messages to the brief. No personal Gmail is involved.
- **Cloudflare Email Routing + Email Workers** ([docs](https://developers.cloudflare.com/email-routing/email-workers/)): a Worker `email()` handler receives the envelope, headers and raw MIME stream, parsed with `postal-mime`. It can forward or reject. There is no built-in mailbox store; you write parsed items to your own storage (KV or similar). The docs' size example uses 25 MiB; no hard limit is stated on that page.
- **Inbound authentication.** Cloudflare requires inbound mail to pass SPF or carry a valid DKIM signature; mail failing both is rejected ([postmaster docs](https://developers.cloudflare.com/email-routing/postmaster/)). Strict DMARC can break forwarded mail. A few newsletters sent from badly configured domains may silently fail to arrive.
- **Alternatives not researched in depth:** a hosted IMAP mailbox on a custom domain polled by the daily job, or other inbound-parse webhooks. IMAP polling suits a once-a-day batch because there is no always-on endpoint; that is a design suggestion for the build, not a researched finding.
- **Gotchas.** Double opt-in confirmation links must be clicked once, manually, at setup. Some publishers require replying from the subscribing address ([Kill the Newsletter notes this](https://kill-the-newsletter.com/)). Strip unsubscribe links and tracking pixels before content reaches an LLM.

## 4. Kill-the-Newsletter

- Per [kill-the-newsletter.com](https://kill-the-newsletter.com/): each feed gets a generated email address and an Atom feed URL; received newsletters become entries. Confirmation emails also appear as entries.
- **Hosted limits (weak for unattended reliability):** entries older than one month are deleted; older entries may also be deleted when new mail arrives to stay under a size limit; some publishers block the service; senders requiring a reply from the subscribing address are unsupported. Anyone holding the feed identifier can unsubscribe you or send spam, so the feed URL is a secret.
- **Self-hosting.** Source is at [github.com/leafac/kill-the-newsletter](https://github.com/leafac/kill-the-newsletter), MIT licensed. I could not read its deployment guide (it lives in a linked repository), so SMTP and hosting requirements are unverified.
- **Role.** It adds little over tier 2 if we already run an inbound address, since we would parse the email ourselves. It is a quick way to turn a feed-less newsletter into a feed with no code.

## 5. Reliability for an unattended daily run

- **Feeds** are the most reliable tier: a plain HTTP GET with no auth for public feeds. Use conditional GET (ETag/Last-Modified), a descriptive User-Agent, and one fetch per day. Ghost's feed declares `ttl` 60, so one daily poll is well within its stated cadence. Expect silent failures: an author regenerating a beehiiv URL or turning off a Buttondown archive breaks the feed with no notice, so the job should flag sources with no new items for many days.
- **Inbound email** is push-based and durable if the receiving service stores messages or the job polls IMAP, but delivery failures (SPF/DKIM/DMARC) are silent. Add a heartbeat check: flag a normally active newsletter that has not arrived in N days.
- **Hosted Kill-the-Newsletter** is the weakest tier (one-month retention, publisher blocking, single hobbyist maintainer).
- **Scraping** is unreliable (layout changes, bot blocking) and ToS-prohibited on at least Substack and beehiiv. Excluded.

## Recommendation for the spec

1. Per-source config records `type: feed | inbox`, plus the feed URL or the subscribing address.
2. Default to `feed` for Substack, Ghost and Buttondown free content.
3. Use `inbox` for paid issues, beehiiv publications without a discoverable feed, and anything else without a working feed.
4. One daily poll with conditional GET for feeds; parse MIME for inbox items; normalise both into one item shape with a canonical URL for hot-trend linking.
5. Stale-source flags for both tiers.
